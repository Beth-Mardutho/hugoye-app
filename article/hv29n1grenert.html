    ABSTRACT

    What is the relationship between the celestial and earthly churches? This article analyzes Bar Hebraeus’s ranking of angels and his analysis of the role of the priesthood in his Candelabra V and Candelabra VI. It is an exploration of his use of sacerdotal imagery in the angelic hierarchy, as well as an analysis of the sources that shape his understanding of the priesthood. Bar Hebraeus sees the ranks of angels as priests organized in three celestial churches, continuous with the human church.     Earthly Priesthood

    The idea of angelic priesthood is also infused in Bar Hebraeus’s understanding of the earthly priesthood’s function. While I will focus here on the theological backing for the function of the priesthood, it is important to note in his treatise on the earthly priesthood, 4455 of 5542 words in the treatise are dedicated explicitly to the sacraments: consecration of different offices (such as priests and deacons), consecration of the chrism, theology and practice of baptism and eucharist, rituals related to the “sleeping”. I will primarily focus on the first section of his treatise, where he discusses what the priesthood is. Obviously, the priests are the people who engage in all of these rituals – who conduct the business of the church. However, Bar Hebraeus specifically addresses the place of the priesthood in the cosmos, which is what I analyze here.

    He begins by exploring what defines priesthood. The entire priesthood, regardless of rank, performs one single operation: “its operation is guiding humans towards similarity to God, as much as is possible for human nature, through the mediation of the similarity of intellectual essences.”78 Thus, as much as is humanly possible, the basic work of the priesthood is to bring people into similarity of God through its mediation. This happens “by (1) the comprehension of contemplative thoughts and (2) by the adornment of these customs which are for our spiritual churches, and (3) by mediation of similarity which is in the conceivable essence, [the priesthood does its work].”79

    CONCLUSION

He does not, in contrast to contemporary writers, discuss schisms within the church and instead discusses one earthly priesthood.108 Bar Hebraeus closely reads and follows Dionysius, integrating Syriac Dionysian thought and liturgical commentary tradition into a new synthesis.
