    3. HISTORICAL CLASSIFICATION OF THE SOCALLED “TRITHEISTS” IN JOHN OF EPHESUS

    The so-called “Tritheistic” movement has its origins in John Askotzanges (Ἀσκοζαγγής), a Miaphysite from Apamea in Syria.30 Michael the Syrian asserts that he was the originator of Tritheism, thereby attributing to him the responsibility for the theological crisis within Miaphysitism in the second half of the sixth century.31 Consequently, John Askotzanges is regarded as a foundational figure in the genesis of Tritheism, signifying the onset of its “archaic phase” (557–567) in Constantinople.32

This nature of the incarnate God-Logos is the one that became manifest in Christ as a hypostatic individual through the Incarnation, and it differs from the divine nature shared by the Father and the Holy Spirit. The Incarnation of God is thus the key to understanding Philoponus’ trinitarian speculations. The divine hypostasis is manifested incarnate nature of the God-Logos. Philoponus derives insights about the Trinity of God from this Miaphysite Christology and seeks to understand and rethink the general nature of God through the hypostasis of God.58 The misunderstanding of this trinitarian perspective, where Philoponus is presumed to have posited three natures in the sense of three essences and divinities, is evident upon superficial reading of Philoponus’ writings.59

    5. CONCLUSION

    John Philoponus – the intellectual leader of the Cononites and the Athanasians during the time of John of Ephesus – almost certainly did not hold a belief in three gods. Philoponus seems to have been misunderstood by his opponents and wrongly vilified as an inspiration for “Tritheism.” The specific way in which the Cononites conceived the doctrine of the Trinity cannot be determined from their writings, since they explicitly referred to John Philoponus and studied his works. Therefore, the reconstruction of the Cononite Trinitarian doctrine can only be based on Philoponus’s explanations.
